## The problem I work on

**Antibiotic resistance is eroding the usefulness of the drugs we rely on.**

I study **antimicrobial peptides** as a different route to antibacterial discovery, with a particular interest in β-hairpin peptides.

I want to understand how a peptide's **sequence becomes structure, how that structure interacts with bacterial membranes, and how those interactions determine biological activity**.

That means my research rarely stays entirely at the bench or entirely on a computer.

It moves between both.

## What I'm working on now

I am a **Graduate Research Assistant / Doctoral Researcher in the Randall Laboratory at UMKC**, where I investigate synthetic and naturally derived antimicrobial peptides as candidate therapeutics against antibiotic-resistant bacteria.

My current work focuses on **β-hairpin antimicrobial peptides** — understanding their diversity, identifying conserved sequence features, prioritizing candidates computationally, and testing their biological and structural properties experimentally.

My work spans:

**candidate identification → sequence analysis → structural analysis → experimental testing → mechanism**

---

## 01 · Discovering β-hairpin antimicrobial peptides

β-hairpin antimicrobial peptides occur across diverse organisms, but their sequences can differ substantially.

I study that diversity to understand what features define the structural family and which properties may be useful for designing new peptide antibiotics.

My computational work includes:

- multiple sequence alignment
- comparative sequence analysis
- phylogenetic reconstruction
- protein sequence analysis
- PDB and UniProt mining
- AlphaFold-based structural analysis
- structure-based candidate prioritization

The goal is not to experimentally test every possible sequence.

It is to make the search **smarter before synthesis begins**.

---

## 02 · Testing what sequence predictions mean biologically

A predicted peptide is only interesting if its biology supports the prediction.

I use experimental approaches to ask whether candidate peptides:

- inhibit bacterial growth
- adopt the expected secondary structure
- interact with biological membranes
- perturb or damage bacterial cells

Methods I work with include:

- bacterial culture
- minimum inhibitory concentration assays
- antimicrobial susceptibility testing
- circular dichroism spectroscopy
- mammalian cell culture
- flow cytometry
- confocal microscopy
- quantitative image analysis with FIJI and CellProfiler

---

## 03 · Connecting sequence to function

One question ties the computational and experimental sides of my research together:

> **Which sequence features make a β-hairpin peptide biologically useful?**

The workflow is iterative.

```text id="d3z5cy"
sequence
   ↓
predicted structure
   ↓
candidate selection
   ↓
experimental testing
   ↓
biological behavior
   ↓
what did the sequence teach us?
   ↓
next design
```

The more we understand this relationship, the more rational peptide discovery can become.

My path into antimicrobial peptide research started with **clinical bacteriology**.

As an undergraduate researcher at Chitwan Medical College, I studied the bacteriological profile of ventilator-associated pneumonia in ICU patients and performed antimicrobial susceptibility testing of clinical isolates.

I later moved into **molecular genetics**, studying *ADH1B* genetic polymorphism and alcohol consumption in the Nepalese population for my master's research.

Today, those backgrounds come together in my work on antimicrobial resistance and peptide-based antibacterial discovery.
